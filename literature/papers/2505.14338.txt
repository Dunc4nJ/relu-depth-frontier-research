                                                Abstract. This work studies the expressivity of ReLU neural networks with a focus on their depth.
                                                A sequence of previous works showed that ⌈log2 (n + 1)⌉ hidden layers are sufficient to compute
                                                all continuous piecewise linear (CPWL) functions on Rn . Hertrich, Basu, Di Summa, and Skutella
                                                (NeurIPS ’21 / SIDMA ’23) conjectured that this result is optimal in the sense that there are CPWL
                                                functions on Rn , like the maximum function, that require this depth. We disprove the conjecture
arXiv:2505.14338v3 [cs.LG] 19 Feb 2026




                                                and show that ⌈log3 (n − 1)⌉ + 1 hidden layers are sufficient to compute all CPWL functions on Rn .
                                                    A key step in the proof is that ReLU neural networks with two hidden layers can exactly represent
                                                the maximum function of five inputs. More generally, we show that ⌈log3 (n − 2)⌉ + 1 hidden layers
                                                are sufficient to compute the maximum of n ≥ 4 numbers. Our constructions almost match the
                                                ⌈log3 (n)⌉ lower bound of Averkov, Hojny, and Merkert (ICLR ’25) in the special case of ReLU
                                                networks with weights that are decimal fractions. The constructions have a geometric interpretation
                                                via polyhedral subdivisions of the simplex into “easier” polytopes.

In words, every CPWL function on Rn can be represented with ⌈log2 (n + 1)⌉ hidden layers. The
reason is that pairwise maxima can be represented with one hidden layer via MAX2 (x1 , x2 ) =
ReLU(x2 − x1 ) + x1 . Then, MAXn+1 —and therefore also the representation (1)—can be realized
with ⌈log2 (n + 1)⌉ hidden layers by implementing it in a binary tree manner.
   From this discussion, it follows that the depth complexity of CPWLn is essentially equal to the
depth complexity of MAXn+1 .1 This highlights the importance of MAXn , and motivates the study of
its depth complexity.
   Summarizing, every CPWL function on Rn can be realized with at most ⌈log2 (n + 1)⌉ hidden
layers, leading to the natural question of whether this upper bound is sharp. In fact, to the best of
our knowledge, the best lower bound is 2, meaning that there is no function known that provably
requires more than two hidden layers.
   Hertrich, Basu, Di Summa, and Skutella [HBDSS23] conjectured that the ⌈log2 (n)⌉ upper bound
on the required number of hidden layers for MAXn is sharp. This conjecture led to a sequence
of works that proved it in a variety of special cases. Mukherjee and Basu [MB17] are the first
to prove the conjecture for the case n = 3, i.e., showing that two hidden layers are necessary
for MAX3 . Haase, Hertrich, and Loho [HHL23] proved the conjecture for general n when the
ReLU network is allowed to use only integer weights. The techniques from [HHL23] were extended
by Averkov, Hojny, and Merkert [AHM25], who proved logarithmic lower bounds for the case
of fractional weights whose denominators do not have some fixed prime factor. The results of
Valerdi [Val24] imply that the conjecture holds for the input convex neural networks (ICNNs)
model, which was defined by Amos, Xu and Kolter [AXK17] and studied in subsequent works
(e.g. [BSA+ 21, CSZ19, CSZ20, BSG+ 23, HL24]). Stronger lower bounds for ICNNs and monotone
ReLU networks were shown by Bakaev, Brunck, Hertrich, Reichman, and Yehudayoff [BBH+ 25].
Grillo, Hertrich, and Loho proved an Ω(log log n) lower bound for ReLU networks that “respect the
singularities of MAXn ” (see [GHL25] for a formal definition). Their proof also implies the conjecture
for n = 5 for networks that “respect the singularities of MAXn ”, a statement that was already shown
in a computer-aided manner in [HBDSS23].

   We hope that our result initiates follow-up work on finding the true required depth to represent
all CPWL functions. There is a gap to close between 2 and ⌈log3 (n − 1)⌉ + 1 hidden layers. While
we resolve the previously open question of whether MAX5 can be represented with two hidden layers,
it intriguingly remains open whether the same is possible for MAX6 .
   We mention a similarity to the computational complexity of multiplying two n × n matrices. The
trivial algorithm runs in time O(n3 ). In his seminal work, Strassen showed that multiplying two
2 × 2 matrices can be done with seven multiplications instead of eight [Str69]. This finite-dimensional
improvement leads to a better algorithm with running time O(nlog2 7 ) ≈ O(n2.8 ). A long line of works
(see [CU03, Cop82, GU18] and the many references within) found better and better upper bounds on
the matrix multiplication exponent ω and the current best upper bound is ω ≤ 2.371552 [ADW+ 25].
Coming back to the maximum function, thinking of the depth parameter d as fixed, we can trivially
compute the maximum of 2d numbers with d hidden layers. The finite-dimensional Proposition 3
leads to a polynomial improvement. With d hidden layers, we can compute the maximum of ≈ (5/2)d
numbers. With a more technical analysis this can be boosted to ≈ 3d . For the matrix multiplication
exponent, we have the trivial lower bound ω ≥ 2. On the other hand, to the best of our knowledge it
could be the case that two hidden layers suffice for computing the maximum of n numbers for all n.


1.2. Universal approximation and required size. Our paper is concerned with the study of
exact expressivity. The celebrated universal approximation theorems (see [Cyb89, HSW89] for initial
versions and [LLPS93] for a version that encompasses ReLU) state that every continuous function on
a bounded domain can be approximated with one hidden layer networks. Related to this, Safran,
Reichman, and Valiant [SRV24] studied how to efficiently approximate MAXn . Nevertheless, for a
rigorous mathematical understanding of neural networks, it is inevitable to study the precise set of
functions representable by different network architectures. Also, universal approximation theorems
only work on bounded domains and might require a large number of neurons. In contrast, it is
easy to verify that our construction needs only linearly many neurons to compute MAXn . It is,
however, more tricky to determine the required size to represent an arbitrary CPWL function with
our construction, as this depends on the number s of terms in the representation (1). The best
known upper bound on s was investigated in a series of works [HLXZ20, HBDSS23, CGR22, BGH25]
and is s ∈ O(pn+1 ), where p is the number of different affine functions represented locally by the
given CPWL function. Another related stream of research is how one can trade depth and size
against each other, with several results implying that mild increases in depth often allow significantly
smaller neural networks [MPCB14, Tel16, ES16, ABMM18, EG24, SES22].

  Even though it is not necessary for the proof of our main theorem, in this section we describe the
geometric intuition behind our constructions. The geometric perspective may help to understand
the constructions and may be useful in future research. The connection to geometry is based on a
well-known fundamental equivalence between (positively homogeneous and convex) CPWL functions

and polytopes (see [ZNL18, MS21, MCT21, HBDSS23, HMST23, HHL23, BGH25, HL24, Val24] and

3.1. Polytopes, support functions, and neural networks. A polytope is the convex hull of
finitely many points in Rn . We refer to [Grü03] for standard terminology and theory of polytopes.
Each polytope P can be uniquely associated with its support function
                                   hP (x) = max{⟨x, p⟩ : p ∈ P }.
The support function hP is convex, positively homogeneous and CPWL. Conversely, for each such
function f there exists a unique polytope P with f = hP , which is sometimes called the Newton
polytope of f , referring to the corresponding concept in tropical geometry.
   The Newton polytope of MAXn is the (n − 1)-dimensional simplex ∆n−1 = conv{e1 , . . . , en } in
Rn , where ei is the i’th standard unit vector. Understanding the ReLU depth complexity of MAXn
translates to a question about generating ∆n−1 in the following model (see e.g. [HBDSS23, Val24]).
For two sets P, Q ⊆ Rn , their Minkowski sum is defined as
                                  P + Q = {p + q : p ∈ P, q ∈ Q}.
Their convex hull is denoted by
                                       P ∗ Q = conv(P ∪ Q).
Denote by P 0 the set of polytopes consisting of a single point (for a fixed dimension n). For k ≥ 0,

where the sum over i is finite. This leads to a computational complexity measure for polytopes. The

   This complexity measure does not, however, capture ReLU depth complexity. The missing piece
is formal Minkowski difference. A polytope X is a formal Minkowski difference of P and Q if

differences of polytopes in P k for the smallest k possible. Understanding the ReLU depth complexity
of the maximum function—and hence of all CPWL functions—boils down to understanding formal
Minkowski differences.

3.2. Upper bounds on the depth via subdivisions. By Lemma 8, we wish to write ∆n−1 as a
formal Minkowski difference. We need to construct two polytopes P, Q ∈ P k for some small k, and
show that ∆n−1 + P = Q. This task is not so easy because P and Q should be somehow “coupled”
for this equation to hold. We now explain how to replace the pair (P, Q) by a subdivision of ∆n−1 .
The advantage is that subdivisions are easier to comprehend and hence to find.

   The proof of Lemma 9 is quite deep. Like the inclusion-exclusion formula, it is tempting to prove
it by induction. But as opposed to the inclusion-exclusion formula, if we remove a single polytope
Qi then the relevant union may no longer be a polytope and the induction hypothesis can not be
applied. We must significantly strengthen the lemma in order to prove it. The proof is obtained in
several steps. First, it uses the equivalence between polytopes and their support functions. Second, it
extends the support function to the lattice of finite unions of polytopes. This extension is topological
and uses the Euler characteristic. Third, the Euler characteristic is shown to be fully additive.
Finally, we can now deduce that the extended support function is fully additive, and hence the
identity map on polytopes is fully additive (the identity map may not be fully additive on the lattice
of finite unions of polytopes).
   Lemma 9 is useful in combination with Lemma 8 because from a cover of X = ∆n−1 by polytopes
Q1 , . . . , Qm , we can write ∆n−1 as a formal Minkowski difference between two polytopes that are as
complex as the QS ’s. This is captured by the following lemma.
Lemma 10. The required number of hidden layers          for MAXn is at most the minimum k so that
there are polytopes Q1 , . . . , Qm so that ∆n−1 = i∈[m] Qi where QS ∈ P k for all non-empty S ⊂ [m].
                                                  S

   Observe that the condition QS ∈ P k holds if Q1 , . . . , Qm ∈ P k and each QS is a face of some
Qi because P k is closed under taking faces. This observation suggests the following way of using
Lemma 10. Instead of writing ∆n−1 as a formal Minkowski difference, it suffices to subdivide ∆n−1
into Q1 , . . . , Qm ∈ P k . If the subdivision is polyhedral, the intersections QS are always faces, and
such a subdivision automatically leads to writing ∆n−1 as ∆n−1 + P = Q for P, Q ∈ P k . Note that
P and Q themselves might be pretty complicated and “hard to find” because they originate from the
exponentially large alternating sums in Lemma 9.

3.4. Higher dimensions. The geometric approach also allows to see why we can compute MAX3n +2
in n + 1 layers. We have seen that ∆3 can be subdivided into four pieces, each of which is a join 2 of
a point pi and a parallelogram Zi . The simplex ∆3 is the join of a point and a triangle. In other
words, we replaced a triangle (which is in P 2 ) with a parallelogram (which is in P 1 ). More abstractly,
we replaced three points (the triangle) by a single parallelogram, which is the geometric intuition
behind Claim 5. This suggests that each layer can reduce “the number of objects to be joined” by a
factor of three, which should then allow ∆n to be computed in roughly log3 (n) layers. A formal
implementation of this proof strategy can be done, but it requires a non-trivial reasoning on the
interactions between joins and subdivisions. The algebraic proof presented in Section 2.2 “hides” this
complex interaction in the vector space structure of Ta,b .

                                                 4. Conclusions
   Summarizing, we provide the first improvement of the upper bound on the required depth to
represent CPWL functions, after the initial representation result by Arora, Basu, Mianjy, and
Mukherjee [ABMM18]. Thereby, we break the log2 (n)-barrier that was conjectured to be opti-
mal [HBDSS23] and proven to be tight in many special cases [HHL23, Val24, GHL25]. Consequently,
our result has the potential to shift the focus of future work from lower bounds to upper bounds,
from impossibility results to constructions. One interpretation of our result is that neural networks
can compute exact maxima more efficiently than in a pairwise manner. Besides the result itself, a
key contribution of our paper is that new upper bounds can be proven via subdividing the simplex
into polytopes with known depth complexity, which is captured by Lemma 10.
   Curiously, while we were able to give a positive answer to the previously open question of whether
the maximum of five numbers can be computed with two hidden layers, the same question remains
open for the maximum of six numbers. The lower bound by Averkov, Hojny, and Merkert [AHM25]
for neural networks with rational weights, however, tells us that further improving the upper
bound would require rational weights with large denominators (or even irrational weights) in the
neural network. Geometrically, when applying our subdivision approach, this would correspond to
subdivisions of the simplex into even smaller cells. Overall, the question of whether constant depth,
perhaps even two hidden layers, suffices to represent all CPWL functions remains an intriguing
mystery.
