                                              On the expressivity of sparse maxout networks

                                                       Moritz Grillo                                Tobias Hofmann

                                              Abstract. We study the expressivity of sparse maxout networks, where each
                                              neuron takes a fixed number of inputs from the previous layer and employs
                                              a, possibly multi-argument, maxout activation. This setting captures key
                                              characteristics of convolutional or graph neural networks. We establish a du-
                                              ality between functions computable by such networks and a class of virtual
                                              polytopes, linking their geometry to questions of network expressivity. In par-
                                              ticular, we derive a tight bound on the dimension of the associated polytopes,
                                              which serves as the central tool for our analysis. Building on this, we con-
                                              struct a sequence of depth hierarchies. While sufficiently deep sparse maxout
                                              networks are universal, we prove that if the required depth is not reached,
                                              width alone cannot compensate for the sparsity of a fixed indegree constraint.

                                              Keywords. Expressivity of neural networks, maxout networks, polyhedral
                                              geometry, virtual polytopes

                                         1 Introduction

                                         Despite the impressive progress that has been made in the practical use of neural net-
                                         works, our theoretical understanding of their inner mechanisms and why they generalize
                                         so well is still far from being complete. Universal approximation theorems, such as those
                                         of Cybenko [1989], Hornik et al. [1990], or Leshno et al. [1993] marked major steps to-
                                         wards explaining the expressivity of neural networks. Yet, making precise what neural
                                         networks can or cannot do goes beyond approximation results and requires addressing
                                         questions of exact representability. That is, given a specific neural network architecture,
                                         what is the exact class of functions that can be represented by that architecture? Re-
                                         cent work, for example, Telgarsky [2016], Arora et al. [2018], or Hertrich et al. [2021],
                                         have provided new insights into how architectural parameters such as depth and width
                                         influence expressivity.



                                                                                     1
In contrast, this article investigates how network sparsity affects expressivity and how it
interacts with architectural parameters such as depth and width. Beyond that, we are
interested in the effects of using multi-argument maxout activation functions, which can
be seen as generalized rectified linear units, as introduced by Goodfellow et al. [2013].
We call a network sparse, or more specifically indegree-d-constrained, if each of its neu-
rons depends on a fixed number d of outputs from the previous layer. This framework
interpolates between fully connected architectures (d equal to the previous layer’s width)
and extremely sparse ones (d = 2). A precise formalization of the architecture we are
concerned with follows in Section 2. Our considerations are motivated by two aspects.
First, one might find it interesting that explicit attempts to construct neural networks of
minimum depth, such as those by Arora et al. [2018] or the recent improvement by Bakaev
et al. [2025] are nonetheless sparse. In other words, when not restricting the width of the
neural network, sparse networks can compute any continuous piecewise linear function
with logarithmic depth.
Second, sparsity is a characteristic property of many practically relevant network types.
For example, a convolutional layer involving a typical 3×3 kernel is indegree-9-constrained.
Another example are graph neural networks, which, depending on the specific variant,
often come with indegree constraints stemming from their localized feature update rules.
The preceding examples also provide relevant network types in which indegree-constrained
operations are used together with pooling steps. In our model, this is reflected by incor-
porating multi-argument maxout activations.
Note also that the architecture we investigate allows for varying indegree constraints
across layers. The only exception is the first and last layer, where indegree constraints
are not imposed. Thus, our model interpolates between fully connected networks on those
in which each neuron depends on only two inputs from the previous layer, apart from the
first and last one. Not enforcing indegree constraints in the first and last layer has the
following reasons.
From a practical perspective, even networks that otherwise incorporate sparse operations,
such as convolutional or graph neural networks often bundle information via fully con-
nected layers into one or few neurons in the final layer. See Krizhevsky et al. [2012] for a
prominent example. Allowing the first layer to be fully connected can be seen as allowing
a general feature transformation or preprocessing step, for instance, in which an image is
patched or graph nodes are embedded into a suitable feature space.
Another reason is that our theoretical interest lies more on structural effects of sparsity
across the layers of a network, rather than on the information-theoretic limitations im-
posed by a restricted receptive field in the first layer. For example, given an input with n
pixels and a network in which each neuron, including those in the first layer, depend on
at most two inputs from the preceding layer, it is clear that a network with depth smaller
than log2 (n) is insufficient to capture every aspect of the input. But this says little about
structural effects within the network, but merely reflects the restrictive reception in the
beginning of information processing chain.




                                              2
Our Contributions. Using a duality between positively homogeneous convex continu-
ous piecewise linear functions and polytopes, we establish an isomorphism between func-
tions computed by sparse maxout networks and a class of virtual polytopes (Theorem 7),
which are formal differences of polytopes. We then study the dimensions of these virtual
polytopes in Section 4, where Theorem 11 provides an upper bound and Theorem 12
shows that this bound is attained. These results form the foundation of our analysis of
how sparsity affects expressivity in Section 5.
While our general framework, introduced in Section 2, allows for varying indegree and
rank constraints across layers, and thus involves vector-valued parameters, the following
outline of our main results suppresses this notation for simplicity. For any indegree
constraint d ∈ N, number of arguments r ∈ N in the maxout activation, and input
dimension n, let Nn (d, r, ℓ) denote the class of functions representable by such networks
with ℓ hidden layers. Our main results are as follows:
   • For any d, r, and ℓ, there exist an input dimension n and a function f : Rn → R
     computable by a fully connected network with two hidden layers and width O(n)
     that is not contained in Nn (d, r, ℓ) (Theorem 18). This, in particular, implies that
     sparse shallow networks cannot represent all continuous piecewise linear functions,
     regardless of their width.
   • For d = r = 2, we fully characterize the class of functions Nn (2, 2, ℓ) (Theorem 19),
     showing a sharp separation for each ℓ ∈ {1, . . . , ⌈log2 (n + 1)⌉}.
In words, when allowing arbitrary input dimension, for any fixed depth and indegree
constraint, the resulting sparsity cannot be compensated by increasing width. This, in
particular, also implies that for a fixed indegree, there is no depth that fully compensates
for sparsity. Overall, our results demonstrate that sparsity has a decisive impact on the
expressivity of neural networks.


Further Related Work An extensive body of research has examined the effect of
depth Hertrich et al. [2021], Haase et al. [2023], Averkov et al. [2025], Grillo et al. [2025],
Bakaev et al. [2025] as well as the relationship between depth and size in neural networks,
showing that deeper architectures can represent certain functions exponentially more
compactly than shallow ones Montúfar et al. [2014], Telgarsky [2016], Arora et al. [2018],
Ergen and Grillo [2024]. Another perspective considers bounds on the total size of the
networks Hertrich and Skutella [2023], Hertrich and Sering [2024], Chen et al. [2022],
Brandenburg et al. [2025].
A related methodological development connects questions of expressivity to Newton poly-
topes of functions realized by neural networks through tropical geometry. This approach
was initiated by Zhang et al. [2018] and further developed by Maragos et al. [2021]. It has
since been applied to the study of decision boundaries, depth and size lower bounds, the
number of linear regions, and approximation capabilities Montúfar et al. [2022], Misiakos
et al. [2022], Haase et al. [2023], Brandenburg et al. [2024], Valerdi [2024], Koutschan
et al. [2023], Hertrich and Loho [2025], Balakin et al. [2025].

2 Sparse networks and continuous piecewise linear functions

A polyhedron P is the intersection of finitely many closed halfspaces and a polytope is
a bounded polyhedron. A hyperplane supports P if it bounds a closed halfspace con-
taining P , and any intersection of P with such a supporting hyperplane yields a face F
of P . A polyhedral complex P is a finite collection of polyhedra such that (i) ∅ ∈ P,
(ii) if P ∈ P, then all faces of P are in P, and (iii) if P, Q ∈ P, then P ∩ Q is a face
both of P and Q. For additional background on polyhedral geometry, we refer to Schri-
jver [1998] and Ziegler [2012]. A continuous function f : Rn → R is called continuous
piecewise linear if there exists a polyhedral complex P such that the restriction of f to
each full-dimensional polyhedron P ∈ P is an affine function. For a convex continuous
piecewise linear function f , there is a unique coarsest such polyhedral complex P, that
is, f is affine within each maximal P ∈ P, but not within Q ⊋ P for any P ∈ P n . We
call P the polyhedral complex underlying f
A function f : Rn → R is called positively homogeneous if f (λx) = λf (x) for any λ ≥ 0.
We denote by Cn the vector space of all positively homogeneous continuous piecewise
linear functions from Rn to R and by Cn+ the set of convex positively homogeneous con-
tinuous piecewise linear functions.

3 Virtual polytopes and support functions

The goal of this section is to establish a duality between specific virtual polytopes and
the functions computable by related sparse maxout networks. For on overview of virtual
polytopes, we refer to Panina and Streinu [2015].
The Minkowski sum of two sets X, Y ∈ Rn is X +Y = {x+y : x ∈ X, y ∈ Y }. By span(X)
we denote the smallest linear subspace containing X, by aff(X) we denote the smallest
affine subspace containing X, and by conv(X) we denote the convex hull of X. A polytope
P is the convex hull of finitely many points in Rn . We denote by Pn the set of all such
polytopes. The dimension of a polytope P is the dimension of its affine hull dim(aff(P )).
Note that P + Q + (−Q) ̸= P for −Q := {−q : q ∈ Q} and hence naive subtraction is not
the inverse of Minkowski addition. But there is a canonical way to introduce a formal
Minkowski difference. The set of polytopes together with Minkowski addition forms a
commutative semigroup S, having the polytope that consists only of the zero vector as
neutral element. For any polytopes P, Q, R in Pn , there holds the cancellation law, which

group of S by Vn := {(P, Q) : P, Q ∈ Pn }/ ∼ where the equivalence relation ∼ is defined
by
                     (P1 , Q1 ) ∼ (P2 , Q2 ) ⇐⇒ P1 + Q2 = P2 + Q1 .

Proof. To check that the given map is well-defined, note first that support functions are
positively homogeneous and continuous piecewise linear. So fP −Q = fP − fQ is also
positively homogeneous and continuous piecewise linear, for any polytopes P and Q.
We yet have to show that fP −Q is independent of the representation of P − Q. Given
equivalent virtual polytopes P − Q and R − S, we obtain P + S = R + Q and hence
fP −Q (x) = fP (x) + fS (x) = fR (x) + fQ (x) = fR−S (x).
To see that the map P − Q 7→ fP (x) − fQ (x) is injective, let fP −Q (x) = fR−S (x) for
all x ∈ Rn . Rearranging gives fP (x) + fS (x) = fR (x) + fQ (x). Since the support function
uniquely determines a polytope, it follows that P + S = R + Q. Thus, the virtual
polytopes P − Q and R − S are the same, as is to be shown.

Discussion

All in all, our work shows that the virtual polytopes associated with the functions com-
putable by sparse maxout networks can help understanding them. We have seen that
our bound on their dimension provides a useful tool for analyzing expressivity, yielding
various depth hierarchies and showing that width cannot fully compensate for given in-
degree constraints. The lineality space of a positively homogeneous continuous piecewise
linear function f : Rn → R is the vector space {v ∈ Rn | f (x) = f (v + x) for all x ∈ Rn }.
The codimension of the lineality space of a function f equals the dimension of the virtual
polytope Vf . The codimension of the lineality space was also used by Koutschan et al.
[2023] to find the smallest k such that the function f is contained in the vector space
Mn (k). Consequently, by tracking the lineality space of a function computed by a sparse
network, one could alternatively derive Theorem 16. However, formulating Lemma 10 di-
rectly in terms of the lineality space, rather than in the dual setting of virtual polytopes,
seems to be more involved.
