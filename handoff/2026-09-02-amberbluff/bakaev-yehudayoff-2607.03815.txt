                                 1. Introduction
1.1. Measures of symmetry. A set K ⊂ Rn is centrally symmetric if there is
o ∈ Rn such that x ∈ K − o ⇐⇒ −x ∈ K − o. Measures of symmetry capture how
close a given convex set is to being centrally symmetric, and are (assumed to be)
affine invariant. The theory of measures of symmetry for convex sets was initiated
by Grünbaum [Grü63]. The recent book by Toth [Tot15] gives a comprehensive
account of this topic. The standard measure of symmetry is the Minkowski measure
which is defined as follows.

Definition. For a convex body K ⊂ Rn and o ∈ int(K), the Minkowski symmetry
of K at o ∈ Rn is
                    mK (o) := inf{α ≥ 1 : −(K − o) ⊆ α(K − o)}.
The Minkowski measure (of symmetry) of K is
                             m∗ (K) :=     inf      mK (o).
                                         o∈int(K)

  The infimum defining m∗ (K) is attained, and any minimizer is called a Minkowski
center.
  The following theorem gives an equivalent description of mK (o) in terms of slabs
containing K. The support function of K is denoted by hK , and the sphere is
denoted by Sn−1 ⊂ Rn .

Theorem 1 (Section 3.2 in [Tot15]). For a convex body K ⊂ Rn and o ∈ int(K),
the Minkowski symmetry of K at o is
                                                 hK−o (−u)
                            mK (o) = max                   .
                                       u∈Sn−1     hK−o (u)
    We define a non-affine-invariant measure (of symmetry) based on simplices.

1.2. The simplex-based measure. A homothet of K ⊂ Rn is a set of the form
H = x + λK where x ∈ Rn and λ ≥ 0 is called the coefficient of homothety (or size
in short). For a compact convex set K ⊂ Rn , the family of (nonnegative) homothets
of K is
                         HK = {x + λK : x ∈ Rn , λ ≥ 0}.
For a compact convex L ⊂ Rn , define
               λK (L) := inf{λ ≥ 0 : ∃x ∈ Rn such that L ⊂ x + λK}.
In words, it is the smallest size of a homothet of K containing L. If L ⊆ Rn is not
a single point and the interior of K is non-empty then 0 < λK (L) < ∞.

remove a small part of it from a region where it does not touch the boundaries of the
homothets of ∆ and −∆ in which it is inscribed. Evaluating ρ∆ (K) only requires
2(n + 1) support-function values, so for polytopes this quantity is, in many cases,
easy to compute.
   We show that the Minkowski measure can also be described in terms of con-
tainment in simplices. Section 3 shows that the supremum of ρ∆ (K) over all affine
images of ∆ is equal to m∗ (K). In words, an affine-invariant version of ρ∆ is m∗ (K).
This can be viewed as a refinement of Theorem 1. Instead of inscribing K into all
possible slabs, we inscribe it into all possible simplices. Consequently, properties of
ρ∆ yield corresponding properties of the Minkowski measure.

Outer additivity (Section 5). Several of the results above rely on the fact that sim-
plices satisfy the following additivity property. The Minkowski sum of two sets is
A + B = {a + b : a ∈ A, b ∈ B}.
Definition. A convex body K ⊂ Rn is called outer additive if for every L = L1 + L2
where L1 , L2 ⊂ Rn are convex and compact, it holds that
                            λK (L) = λK (L1 ) + λK (L2 ).
6                            BAKAEV AND YEHUDAYOFF

   The additivity of K does not rely on the “position” of K; it is invariant under
invertible affine maps. Because it is always true that λK (L) ≤ λK (L1 ) + λK (L2 ),
the additivity of K is equivalent to λK (L) ≥ λK (L1 ) + λK (L2 ).
   The fact that simplices are outer additive easily follows from the additivity of
support functions. It is natural to ask whether similar properties can be obtained
by replacing the simplex with some other convex body. Section 5 characterizes
simplices as the only outer additive convex bodies.
Theorem 6. A convex body is outer additive iff it is a simplex.

Depth complexity (Section 6). Our final application is motivated by the study of neu-
ral networks (NNs). The study of NNs lead to the following model for constructing
polytopes. Polytopes of depth zero are
                               Pn,0 = {{x} : x ∈ Rn }.
For d > 0, polytopes of depth at most d are

The depth complexity of a polytope P ⊂ Rn is the smallest d such that P ∈ Pn,d . We
find this computational-complexity-like framework for studying polytopes interesting
in its own right, and provide further motivations later on.
Theorem 7. If a polytope P ⊂ Rn has depth complexity d < ⌈log2 (n + 1)⌉, then
                                  ρ∆ (P ) ≤ 2d − 1.
This bound is sharp (this inequality is an equality for some polytopes).
   Consequently, small depth polytopes are, in some sense, close to being symmetric
in the Minkowski measure of symmetry.
Theorem 8. Let P ⊂ Rn be a non-singleton polytope, let k = dim P , and suppose
that P ∈ Pn,d . If 0 ≤ d < ⌈log2 (k + 1)⌉, then
                                  m∗ (P ) ≤ 2d − 1.
   Theorem 7 follows from the following two more informative theorems, which con-
trol the simplex-based measure under the two operations on polytopes we consider.
Theorem 9. If L = L1 +. . .+Lm where L1 , . . . , Lm ⊂ Rn are non-singleton compact
convex sets, then
                      ρ∆ (L) ≤ max{ρ∆ (Lj ) : j ∈ [m]}.

Theorem 10. If L = conv(L1 ∪ L2 ) where L1 , L2 ⊂ Rn are non-singleton compact
convex sets, then
                      ρ∆ (L) ≤ ρ∆ (L1 ) + ρ∆ (L2 ) + 1.
  In words, the theorems state that Minkowski sum does not increase the simplex-
based measure beyond the worst summand, and the convex hull of union of two
polytopes at most doubles (with a possible extra +1). Both theorems are proved in
Section 2.2.
  Because ρ∆ (∆) = n, we can deduce that simplices cannot be approximated by
low-depth polytopes. In Section 6.5, we translate the geometric obstruction given by
ρ∆ (∆) being large to a natural inapproximability statement for h∆ by small depth
input convex neural networks (we mention that h∆ is particularly important in this
area—it is “complete for depth”).

(without using any Minkowski sums).

ral network (ICNN) model for computing convex functions (defined in [AXK17]).
The input gates compute linear functions on Rn . Inner gates compute functions of
                  P
the form g(x) = j cj max{aj (x), bj (x)} where for every j, it holds that cj > 0 and
aj , bj were previously computed. Motivation for studying ICNNs comes from the
facts that ICNNs compute convex functions, and minimizing convex functions is a
tractable goal. For example, ICNNs can be trained to represent a loss function that
guides the training process of other models.
    The correspondence between polytopes and convex functions is seen via support
functions. Every closed convex set P ⊂ Rn defines a support function hP : Rn → R
via
                            hP (x) = max{⟨x, y⟩ : y ∈ P }.
                     A SIMPLEX-BASED MEASURE OF SYMMETRY                          25

Support functions have many important properties (see e.g. [Grü03]). In particu-
lar, there is a one-to-one correspondence between P and hP . This correspondence
translates to the computational setting as the following lemma shows.
Lemma ([HBDSS21]). For every polytope X ∈ Rn , the required number of hidden
layers to compute hX is equal to the minimum d such that there are P, Q ∈ Pn,d
such that
                                  X + P = Q.
   A particularly important polytope in this setting is the simplex. The support
function h∆n corresponds to computing the maximum of n + 1 numbers. The depth
complexity of ∆n is known to be ⌈log2 (n+1)⌉: the upper bound is immediate, and the
matching lower bound was proved by Valerdi [Val24]. This quantity is also important
on the functional side, because if ∆n has depth d, then every continuous piecewise
linear (CPWL) function on Rn−1 can be computed with d hidden layers [ABMM18,
WS05]. In other words, h∆n is complete for depth complexity with respect to all
CPWL functions on (n − 1)-dimensional space. This led the authors of [HBDSS21]
to conjecture that the number of hidden layers required to compute h∆n is exactly
⌈log2 (n+1)⌉. The authors of [BBH+ 26] constructed polytopes P, Q ∈ P4,2 such that
                                   ∆4 + P = Q.
This construction refuted the conjecture. With some additional work, [BBH+ 26]
deduced that the number of hidden layers required to compute h∆n is at most
≈ log3 (n). Other lower-bound results are known under additional restrictions, such
as integral or rational weights [HHL23, AHM25].
6.2. Indecomposability and approximation. There are numerous classical ap-
proximation problems in convex geometry, such as approximating a given convex
set by polytopes and approximating a given polytope by specific polytopes. These
problems, in their many forms, have been studied for many years with various types
of motivation (see [BJ00, Gru83, Grü03, Kal82] and references within).
   Shephard and others considered the following approximation problem (see [She64]
and references within). A target convex set T is approximated by a collection K
of convex sets if there is a sequence of sets (Kj )∞
                                                   j=1 such that each Kj is a finite
Minkowski sum of elements of K and Kj → T as j → ∞ in the Hausdorff metric.
   As we describe next, Shephard proved that an indecomposable T can only be
“trivially approximated”. Recall that HK is the set of all homothets of K. A set
K ⊂ Rn is indecomposable if it is “prime with respect to Minkowski summation”:
Definition. A set K ⊂ Rn is indecomposable if for every L1 , . . . , Lm ⊂ Rn such
that K = L1 + . . . + Lm , it holds that L1 , . . . , Lm ∈ HK .
26                            BAKAEV AND YEHUDAYOFF

   Indecomposable polytopes were studied in many works (see e.g. [FG64, She64,
She63]). It is known, e.g., that if K is a polytope so that all of its two-dimensional
faces are triangles then K is indecomposable [She63]. Indecomposability was also
recently used to study the expressivity of monotone ReLU neural networks and
input convex neural networks (ICNNs); see [BBH+ 25, Val24]. Returning to the
approximation problem mentioned above, Shephard proved that an indecomposable
T can be approximated by a class of convex bodies K only if K contains a homothet
of T .
   It is well-known that simplices are indecomposable. One of our results is a stronger
quantitative obstruction. We show that if for some depth parameter d there is a
sequence of polytopes (Pj ) in Pn,d such that Pj → ∆n as j → ∞, then necessarily
d ≥ ⌈log2 (n + 1)⌉. In fact, we show that polytopes of depth complexity smaller than
⌈log2 (n + 1)⌉ are uniformly bounded away from ∆n .
   More generally, we are interested in the depth complexity of approximating a
given polytope up to some finite accuracy. We measure the quality of an approx-
imation using a “simplex-based” measure. As opposed to Shephard’s result, in
our non-asymptotic setting, some indecomposable polytopes can be approximated
non-trivially. The reason is that, in dimension n ≥ 3, the set of indecomposable
polytopes is dense [Sch13], so every polytope P ⊂ Rn is close to an indecomposable
polytope Q ⊂ Rn (for example, we can build Q that is close to P such that all of its
two-dimensional faces are triangles).
